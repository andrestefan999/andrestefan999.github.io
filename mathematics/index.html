Mathematics Study Tips

Practical strategies to improve your mathematical understanding, develop problem-solving skills and maximise your examination results.

Mathematics is a subject where consistent practice makes a significant difference. However, how you practise is just as important as how much time you spend studying.

Unlike subjects that rely heavily on recalling information, mathematics requires you to apply concepts, recognise patterns and work through unfamiliar problems. Simply reading through worked examples or memorising formulas is rarely enough to develop these skills.

The most effective approach combines regular practice, a strong understanding of mathematical concepts and the ability to learn from your mistakes.

1. Practice, Practice, Practice

Mathematics is a skill that develops through application. Understanding a worked example is very different from being able to solve a similar question independently.

The more questions you attempt, the more familiar you become with mathematical techniques, patterns and the different ways concepts can be applied. Regular practice also helps develop fluency, allowing you to solve familiar problems more efficiently.

However, practising effectively is more important than simply completing as many questions as possible. Make sure you understand the methods you are using rather than blindly repeating procedures.

When learning a new topic, start with straightforward questions to establish your understanding. Gradually progress towards more complex problems that require you to combine multiple concepts.

Try to make mathematics a regular part of your study routine rather than leaving most of your practice until immediately before an examination. Consistent exposure to mathematical problems helps reinforce your understanding over time.

2. Past Papers Are Your Best Friend

Past examination papers are among the most valuable resources available to mathematics students. They provide an opportunity to practise applying your knowledge in a format that closely resembles the actual examination.

Completing past papers helps you become familiar with the types of questions examiners ask, the level of difficulty you can expect and the way different topics are assessed.

They are also particularly useful for identifying gaps in your knowledge. You may feel confident with a topic when completing textbook exercises but struggle when the same concept is presented in a less familiar way.

As your examination approaches, try completing entire past papers under timed conditions. This helps develop your ability to manage time, maintain concentration and work consistently throughout an examination.

Importantly, do not just complete a paper and check your final mark. Spend time reviewing the questions you answered incorrectly, identifying which topics need further revision and understanding how the marking criteria award marks for your working.

Tip: Keep a record of the questions you struggle with in past papers. Revisit them a few days later and attempt them again without looking at the solution. Being able to solve a question independently is a much better indication of understanding than simply recognising its worked solution.

3. Challenge Yourself Outside Your Comfort Zone

It is easy to become comfortable completing questions that you already know how to solve. While these questions are useful for reinforcing your understanding, they do not necessarily prepare you for unfamiliar examination problems.

To develop stronger problem-solving skills, regularly attempt questions that are more challenging than what you are accustomed to.

This might involve attempting harder textbook exercises, extension questions or problems that combine several mathematical concepts. You can also challenge yourself by reducing the amount of time you allow for questions you have previously completed.

Another useful approach is to attempt questions without immediately looking at worked solutions or hints. Give yourself time to experiment with different approaches and work out how the information in the question can be used.

You will not always be able to solve every difficult question, and that is perfectly normal. The important part is understanding where you got stuck and learning how to approach similar problems in the future.

Developing this ability is particularly important in Mathematics Extension 1 and Extension 2, where questions can require considerable independent reasoning and the application of multiple techniques.

4. Don't Neglect the Easy Questions

When preparing for examinations, students often focus heavily on the most difficult questions. Although challenging problems are important, it is equally important not to neglect straightforward questions.

Easy questions provide an opportunity to improve your speed, accuracy and confidence with fundamental mathematical techniques. They also reinforce the basic skills that underpin more complicated problems.

In an examination, straightforward questions are often opportunities to secure marks efficiently. Losing marks through simple calculation errors, incorrect substitutions or poorly written working can have a significant impact on your overall result.

Try to complete familiar questions accurately and efficiently without becoming careless. As you improve, aim to reduce the time required while maintaining the same level of accuracy.

Remember that examination performance is not just about being able to solve the hardest question. It is also about consistently securing the marks available throughout the entire paper.

5. Understand Your Mistakes

One of the most effective ways to improve in mathematics is to understand why you get questions wrong. Simply checking the answer and moving on wastes an opportunity to identify and address weaknesses in your understanding.

When you make a mistake, try to identify exactly where your solution went wrong. Was it a misunderstanding of the concept, an incorrect mathematical technique, an algebraic error or a simple calculation mistake?

Understanding the underlying cause allows you to address the problem rather than repeatedly making the same mistake.

A useful strategy is to maintain a collection of questions that you previously struggled with. This could be a dedicated notebook or a digital document containing the original question, your mistake and the correct approach.

Periodically revisit these questions and attempt them again without referring to the solutions. This helps determine whether you have genuinely addressed the weakness or simply memorised the answer.

Remember: Getting a question wrong is not necessarily a sign that you are bad at mathematics. It is an opportunity to identify something you need to improve. The important part is making sure you learn from it.

6. Don't Look at Solutions Too Early

It can be tempting to look at a worked solution as soon as you encounter a difficult question. Although this might help you understand how the question is solved, it does not necessarily mean you would be able to solve it independently.

Give yourself time to work through challenging questions before looking at the solution. Even if you are unsure how to begin, try identifying the information provided, the result you need to find and which mathematical concepts might be relevant.

Experiment with different approaches and see whether you can make progress. Sometimes a question becomes much easier once you identify the right starting point.

If you eventually need to look at a worked solution, avoid simply reading through every step. Instead, identify the point at which you became stuck and understand why the next step was necessary.

Once you understand the solution, put it away and attempt the question again from the beginning. This is a much better way to check whether you have understood the method.

7. Show All Your Working

Developing the habit of showing clear and organised working is important for both learning mathematics and performing well in examinations.

It can be tempting to perform several calculations mentally and write down only the final answer. However, skipping important steps makes it easier to overlook mistakes and can make it difficult to identify where your reasoning went wrong.

Writing down your working also helps communicate your mathematical reasoning. In examinations, this is particularly important because marks are often awarded for correct methods and intermediate steps, even when the final answer is incorrect.

Try to keep equations aligned, write each important step clearly and avoid unnecessary jumps in your calculations. This makes your solutions easier to follow and gives you a clearer process to check when reviewing your answers.

Over time, organised working also helps develop a more consistent approach to solving complicated problems.

8. Practise a Variety of Questions

Completing large numbers of identical questions can help reinforce a particular technique, but it can also create a false sense of confidence.

When you complete several questions of exactly the same type in succession, you already know which technique is expected. In an examination, however, you are often required to recognise the appropriate approach without being given that indication.

Try mixing different question types and topics within your study sessions. For example, rather than spending an entire session completing differentiation questions, alternate between differentiation, integration, trigonometry and probability.

This encourages you to think more carefully about the structure of each problem and decide which mathematical techniques are appropriate.

It also helps you recognise connections between different topics, particularly when examination questions combine concepts that you have previously studied separately.

9. Revise Older Topics Regularly

Mathematics builds upon previously learned concepts. A strong understanding of algebra, functions and trigonometry, for example, provides a foundation for many more advanced mathematical topics.

One common mistake is to focus exclusively on the topic currently being taught. While it is important to keep up with new material, neglecting older topics can lead to gaps in your knowledge.

Instead, dedicate some of your study time to revisiting material from previous weeks or terms. This might involve completing a handful of questions from an older topic or attempting a mixed revision worksheet.

Regularly revisiting older material helps maintain your mathematical fluency and reduces the amount of content you need to relearn before examinations.

It is also particularly useful for identifying topics that you thought you understood but have gradually become less confident with over time.

10. Understand the Theory Behind the Mathematics

Memorising formulas and learning procedures can help you solve familiar questions, but developing a deeper understanding of mathematical concepts allows you to apply them much more flexibly.

Whenever you learn a new technique, try to understand why it works rather than simply memorising the steps involved.

For example, when learning differentiation, understanding the underlying principles helps explain why different rules can be applied and how they relate to one another. Similarly, understanding the relationship between a function and its derivative makes it easier to interpret and solve unfamiliar problems.

Consider asking yourself questions such as:

  • Why does this mathematical rule work?
  • When can I apply this technique?
  • Are there any restrictions or assumptions?
  • How does this concept relate to other topics?
  • Can I explain the reasoning behind the solution without referring to my notes?

This approach is particularly valuable in the higher levels of Mathematics, where questions increasingly require you to combine concepts and apply your knowledge in unfamiliar situations.

Final Thoughts

Improving in mathematics requires consistency, patience and a willingness to challenge yourself. There is no substitute for regular practice, but the way you approach that practice can make a substantial difference.

Make an effort to attempt unfamiliar problems, use past papers regularly, understand your mistakes and revisit older topics. Equally, do not overlook the importance of mastering fundamental techniques and maintaining accuracy with straightforward questions.

Ultimately, the aim is to develop the ability to solve problems independently rather than simply recognise questions you have seen before.

The more effectively you practise, the better prepared you will be for whatever an examination throws at you.

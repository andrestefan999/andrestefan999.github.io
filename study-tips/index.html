Study Advice

Study Tips

Practical strategies to build better study habits, understand difficult concepts and make your study time more effective.

Studying effectively is about more than how many hours you spend at your desk. The quality of your practice, your approach to learning and your ability to remain consistent all have a significant impact on your results.

Many students spend considerable time studying without making the progress they expect. Repeatedly reading notes, memorising facts or completing familiar questions can feel productive, but these activities do not necessarily develop a deep understanding.

Developing effective study habits requires two things: building a consistent routine and learning how to engage with material effectively.

1. Build Consistent Study Habits

One of the biggest challenges students face is simply getting themselves to study. It is easy to put off difficult tasks, particularly when there is no immediate deadline.

A useful starting point is to establish a clear reason for studying. Whether your goal is to achieve a particular ATAR, gain entry into a university course or develop a deeper understanding of a subject, having a meaningful objective gives you something to work towards.

However, motivation alone is unreliable. Instead of waiting until you feel motivated, aim to develop a routine that makes studying a regular part of your day.

If you currently struggle to study consistently, attempting to spend several hours studying every afternoon is unlikely to be sustainable. Start with a manageable amount of time, perhaps 30 minutes each day, and gradually increase it as the habit becomes established.

It also helps to set clear and achievable goals. Rather than sitting down with the vague intention of studying Mathematics, decide exactly what you want to accomplish. For example, you might revise a particular concept, complete five practice questions or review mistakes from a previous exam.

Allocating approximate time limits to each task provides structure and makes it easier to get started. The Pomodoro technique can also help when motivation is low. This involves working in focused intervals, commonly 25 minutes, followed by a short break.

Ultimately, a sustainable routine is more valuable than occasionally spending an entire afternoon studying before an examination.

2. Practise Solving Unfamiliar Problems

For subjects such as Mathematics and Physics, practice is essential. Understanding a concept in theory is very different from being able to apply it independently to a question.

Repeated practice develops mathematical fluency, reinforces important techniques and makes it easier to recognise patterns. However, simply completing large numbers of familiar questions is not necessarily the most effective approach.

One of the most important skills you can develop is the ability to work through a problem you have never encountered before. Examination questions may combine several concepts, present information in an unfamiliar way or require you to apply your knowledge in a new context.

When approaching an unfamiliar question, start by identifying the information provided and what you are being asked to determine. Consider which concepts might be relevant and how you can connect the information in the question to the result you need.

Even if you cannot solve the entire question, establishing a logical starting point is valuable. This develops your ability to reason independently rather than relying on memorised procedures.

When you encounter a difficult question, do not simply memorise its solution. Try to understand why each step was necessary and identify the key idea that allowed the problem to be solved.

Consider how that same idea might apply to other questions. This allows you to transfer your understanding to a much wider range of problems.

3. Understand Why You Make Mistakes

A common misconception among students is that most examination errors are simply silly mistakes. In reality, many errors result from an underlying weakness in understanding, problem-solving technique or examination habits.

It is important to identify the actual cause rather than simply telling yourself to be more careful.

Skipping intermediate steps, performing too many operations mentally or writing equations in a disorganised manner increases the likelihood of errors. Developing a consistent approach, keeping working organised and writing down important steps can help prevent them.

Other errors occur because students do not understand why a particular mathematical or scientific rule is necessary. For example, when solving an equation by squaring both sides, additional solutions may be introduced that do not satisfy the original equation. Similarly, taking the square root of both sides requires consideration of both positive and negative possibilities.

Even when you understand a concept and use an appropriate method, you can still make errors if you lose concentration or fail to check your work. Checking should be an active part of your problem-solving process.

When reviewing your answers, consider whether:

  • Your answer has the correct units
  • Your result is mathematically or physically reasonable
  • You have answered the actual question
  • Your solution satisfies the original equation or conditions

Remember: Many careless mistakes have identifiable causes. Understanding why an error occurred is more useful than simply repeating the question or telling yourself to be more careful.

4. Develop a Deep Understanding of Content-Based Subjects

For subjects such as Biology and Chemistry, developing a strong underlying understanding is particularly important.

Although remembering key facts is necessary, isolated rote memorisation often produces less durable knowledge and makes it difficult to apply information to unfamiliar situations. Instead, aim to understand why things happen and how different concepts relate to one another.

Whenever you learn a new concept, try to understand the reasoning behind it. Why does this process occur? What causes it? How does it relate to other concepts you have studied?

For example, when learning about a physiological process, do not simply memorise the sequence of events. Consider why each step occurs and what would happen if one part of the process changed.

This creates meaningful connections between ideas, making information easier to understand, remember and apply in different contexts.

5. Use Active Recall and Spaced Repetition

Simply rereading information can create a false sense of familiarity. You might recognise material when looking at it but struggle to recall it independently.

Active recall addresses this by requiring you to retrieve information from memory. Rather than looking at your notes, try explaining a concept in your own words, drawing a process from memory or answering questions about previously studied material.

Spaced repetition complements this approach by revisiting information at gradually increasing intervals. Rather than attempting to learn everything in one session, return to important concepts over several days and weeks.

Combining active recall with spaced repetition helps strengthen long-term retention and makes it easier to retrieve information when you need it.

6. Interleave Your Practice

Another useful learning technique is interleaving. Rather than spending an entire study session practising one type of question, interleaving involves mixing different topics or problem types within a study session.

For example, instead of completing 20 consecutive questions on differentiation, you might alternate between differentiation, integration, trigonometry and probability.

Although this can initially feel more difficult, it encourages you to identify which concepts and techniques are appropriate for each question, rather than simply repeating the same procedure.

This is particularly useful for examination preparation, where questions are rarely grouped in a way that tells you exactly which method to use.

Interleaving works particularly well alongside spaced repetition, allowing you to revisit different concepts while practising how to distinguish between them.

Example: Instead of completing an entire worksheet on one topic, try alternating between several topics you have already studied. This forces you to decide which approach is appropriate for each question.

7. Use Notes Effectively

Note-taking is often poorly utilised. Many students spend considerable time copying information from textbooks, presentations or online resources without actively engaging with the material.

Producing detailed notes can feel productive, but it can also distract from the more important task of understanding what you are learning. Notes can be found online, but developing a genuine understanding of a difficult concept requires much more active engagement.

This does not mean that notes are unnecessary. Rather, they should support your learning instead of becoming the primary learning activity.

When learning something new, focus on understanding the concept before attempting to summarise it. Once you have developed an understanding, concise notes can help consolidate the important ideas.

Mind maps are useful for content-heavy subjects because they show how different concepts connect. Alternatively, writing short summaries in your own words can help identify the most important information without overwhelming yourself with unnecessary detail.

8. Prioritise Sleep and Recovery

Sleep is an important part of learning, not simply time away from studying. It supports memory consolidation, allowing information learned during the day to be strengthened and retained.

Consistently sacrificing sleep to study for longer can therefore be counterproductive. Poor sleep can also impair concentration, attention and problem-solving ability, making subsequent study sessions less effective.

Aim to maintain a consistent sleep schedule and get sufficient sleep each night. Teenagers generally need around 8–10 hours, while adults generally need 7–9 hours.

Rather than staying up late to finish another set of questions, prioritise getting enough rest so that you can approach your next study session with better concentration.

Final Thoughts

Effective studying requires a balance between consistency, active engagement and deliberate practice. Establish a sustainable routine, challenge yourself with unfamiliar problems, learn from your mistakes and make use of techniques such as active recall, spaced repetition and interleaving.

It is easy to measure productivity by the number of hours spent studying or questions completed. However, these measures do not necessarily reflect how much you have learned.

Instead, regularly evaluate your progress:

  • Can you explain concepts without referring to your notes?
  • Can you solve unfamiliar questions independently?
  • Can you identify and correct the causes of previous mistakes?
  • Can you recall information after several days or weeks?

Developing effective study habits takes time. By establishing a sustainable routine and engaging more deeply with the material, you can gradually become a more independent and effective learner.

The goal is not simply to study more. It is to learn more effectively.
